# Personal Finance and Budgeting Guide

## Page 1: Build a monthly plan

This original educational guide uses illustrative numbers, not personalized financial advice. All amounts are in USD. Adapt the examples to your own situation.

Start with take-home income: the amount available after taxes and payroll deductions. List essential expenses, optional spending, and savings separately. A budget is a plan, not a guarantee.

The example monthly take-home income is $3,000. A 50/30/20 allocation divides it into essentials of $1,500, wants of $900, and savings or extra debt payments of $600. These three allocations add up to $3,000.

Essentials can include housing, basic food, utilities, and necessary transport. Wants are optional purchases. Savings can include an emergency fund or a planned future purchase. Minimum debt payments belong in the spending plan; extra debt repayment can use the savings allocation.

Record actual spending each week. At the end of the month, compare planned and actual amounts. If essentials exceed the example's allocation, adjust the plan rather than treating 50/30/20 as a rule that fits everyone.

## Page 2: Create an emergency reserve

An emergency reserve is money set aside for unexpected essential costs or temporary loss of income. Keep it separate from day-to-day spending and accessible when needed.

This guide's illustrative target is three to six months of essential expenses. With monthly essentials of $1,500, the three-month target is $4,500 and the six-month target is $9,000. Use essential expenses, not the full $3,000 take-home income, for this calculation.

Starting from zero and saving the entire $600 monthly savings allocation toward the reserve reaches the three-month target during month 8: seven deposits total $4,200 and eight total $4,800. The six-month target takes 15 deposits of $600. These examples ignore interest and assume no withdrawals.

If the monthly savings allocation is split among several goals, the emergency reserve will take longer. After using reserve money, review the budget and plan how to replenish it.

## Page 3: Plan irregular expenses and debt

Predictable annual costs are different from emergencies. Use a sinking fund: save a small amount each month toward a known future bill.

For an annual insurance bill of $600, set aside $50 per month for 12 months. Keep this reserve separate from the emergency fund, even if both balances are held in one bank account. Starting with no sinking fund one month before the bill is due does not make twelve months of contributions available.

If $50 of the example's $600 savings allocation goes to this annual bill, $550 remains for other goals. At $550 per month, a $4,500 emergency reserve starting from zero takes 9 monthly deposits, ignoring interest and withdrawals. Eight deposits total $4,400, which is not enough.

A separate debt illustration starts with a balance of $1,200. At $200 per month, payoff takes 6 months only if no interest, fees, or new borrowing are added. Real debt may cost more; consult the actual terms. This guide does not specify a recommended investment, lender, or repayment product.

## Page 4: Review and use the guide carefully

Weekly review: record purchases and check remaining category balances. Monthly review: compare actual spending against the plan and adjust next month's allocations. Review predictable annual bills before treating leftover cash as spendable.

Keep emergency reserves, annual-bill sinking funds, and day-to-day spending conceptually separate. The same $50 cannot simultaneously fund an insurance bill and an emergency reserve. When combining the examples on earlier pages, subtract money allocated to one goal before calculating another goal's timeline.

The examples assume stable income and regular monthly deposits. They do not model inflation, investment returns, taxes, interest on savings, or debt interest unless explicitly stated.

This guide does not provide tax brackets, country-specific benefits, investment return forecasts, stock recommendations, credit scores, or legal advice. Questions about those subjects cannot be answered from this document. Use appropriate qualified guidance for personal financial decisions.
